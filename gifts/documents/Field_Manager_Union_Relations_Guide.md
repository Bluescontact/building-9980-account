# Field Manager's Guide to Union Relations

**Recognition Infrastructure Approach**

*Operating from coherence within NEBA-IUEC contract structures*

## Introduction: Recognition Infrastructure

This guide operates from a fundamentally different premise than traditional labor-management adversarial models. Recognition Infrastructure treats structural conflicts not as battles to be won, but as information about system functioning.

**Core Principle:**

Union representatives are not obstacles to navigate around—they're partners operating within their own constraints. Your job isn't to defeat them; it's to understand what constraints they're operating under and find paths that work for everyone.

## Section 1: Understanding the Union Representative's Position

### What Business Agents Actually Navigate

**1. Multiple Constituencies**

- Members (with competing needs and perspectives)
- Local Union leadership and politics
- International Union requirements and oversight
- Building trades council relationships
- Their own re-election concerns

**2. Structural Constraints**

- Contract language they inherited, didn't write
- Past practice precedents that limit flexibility
- Grievance settlements that created expectations
- Political capital limited by recent wins/losses
- International Representative oversight

**3. Information Asymmetries**

- They often hear member complaints first
- They may not understand company operational constraints
- They may have incomplete information about a situation
- They're making decisions with partial data

**Recognition Pattern:**

When a BA seems unreasonable, that's information about which of these constraints are binding. Your job is to figure out what they're actually optimizing for, then find solutions that address those constraints.

## Section 2: The Four Movements in Union Relations

### Movement 1: DIFFERENTIATION — Maintaining Your Position

**What this means:**

You have legitimate operational needs. The BA has legitimate representational duties. Neither of you needs to pretend the other's position is invalid.

**In Practice:**

DON'T: "You're being unreasonable."
DO: "I understand you need to protect seniority rights. I also need to staff this emergency callback. Let's look at the contract language on temporary transfers."

DON'T: Cave immediately to avoid conflict
DO: "This is where my constraint is. Help me understand yours."

### Movement 2: CONNECTION — Genuine Contact with What's Present

**What this means:**

Stop anticipating what the BA will say. Stop preparing your defensive response. Actually listen to what constraint they're naming.

**In Practice:**

When BA says: "This violates past practice"
Don't hear: "They're just being difficult"
Actually hear: "There's a precedent I'm worried about setting"

Then respond to what's actually there: "What's the precedent concern? Let's see if we can structure this differently."

### Movement 3: BOUNDARIES — Limits as Information

**What this means:**

Both sides have real limits. Making them visible isn't failure—it's the beginning of problem-solving.

**In Practice:**

"Here's my constraint: Customer requires this certification by Friday or we lose the contract. What's your constraint?"

DON'T hide your constraints or try to manipulate
DO make the actual structure visible so both parties can solve against it

### Movement 4: ARCHITECTURE — Building Capacity

**What this means:**

Every interaction either builds or degrades the capacity for future cooperation. Choose building.

**In Practice:**

After resolving an issue: "This worked. Should we document this approach for next time?" "What made this solution work for you? I want to understand it for future reference."

Think in terms of: Does this exchange make the next one easier or harder?

## Section 3: Common Scenarios & Recognition Infrastructure Responses

### Scenario 1: Work Assignment Dispute

**Traditional Management Response:**

"Article IV gives us the right to assign work. This is our decision."

**Recognition Infrastructure Response:**

"Help me understand what you're seeing. I'm reading Article IV as allowing this assignment, but I want to understand what concern you have about it."

**Why this works:**

You're not caving. You're gathering information. Often the BA is worried about something specific (setting precedent, member perception, political optics) that you can address without changing the core decision.

### Scenario 2: Safety Delay vs. Production Pressure

**Traditional Management Response:**

"OSHA allows us to proceed. The union is just slowing things down."

**Recognition Infrastructure Response:**

"Article XXVIII gives employees the right to refuse work they believe is unsafe. You're representing someone exercising that right. Let's document what specific condition they're concerned about, then determine together what would resolve it."

**Why this works:**

You're acknowledging the contractual right, treating the concern as legitimate data, and creating a path forward. The BA now has to work with you to define what would constitute resolution—they can't just say "unsafe" without specifics.

### Scenario 3: Grievance Over Discipline

**Traditional Management Response:**

"The discipline was justified. We're going to arbitration."

**Recognition Infrastructure Response:**

"I understand you're filing a grievance under Article XV. Before we go through the full process, help me understand what outcome would resolve this. What's the real issue?"

**Why this works:**

Many grievances are filed because the employee feels unheard, or the BA is showing they're "doing something." Understanding what would actually resolve it often reveals solutions that don't require arbitration. You're not weakening your position—you're gathering information about what matters.

## Section 4: Navigating the NEBA Agreement Structure

### Key Articles Field Managers Must Know

**Article IV — Work Jurisdiction**

- What it covers: What work is covered by the agreement
- Your leverage: Clear definitions of scope
- BA's leverage: Exclusive jurisdiction language
- Recognition approach: When disputes arise, both parties should be able to point to specific paragraph language

**Article XV — Arbitration**

- What it covers: Grievance procedure and timelines
- Your leverage: Strict timelines apply to both sides
- BA's leverage: Can take disputes to impartial arbitration
- Recognition approach: Use the structured process as a container for disagreement. The timelines aren't obstacles—they're agreed-upon rhythms for resolution.

**Article XXII — Hiring, Layoffs, Transfers**

- What it covers: Employment processes and seniority
- Your leverage: Right to reject unsuitable referrals
- BA's leverage: Hiring hall integrity and seniority rights
- Recognition approach: This is where most daily friction occurs. Make your operational needs visible; understand their political constraints around fairness.

**Article XXVIII — Safety**

- What it covers: Safety procedures and worker rights
- Your leverage: OSHA compliance requirements
- BA's leverage: Work refusal rights under good faith belief
- Recognition approach: Safety conflicts are rarely actually about safety—they're about trust, communication, and documentation. Focus on making the actual hazard assessment visible.

## Section 5: When Recognition Infrastructure Isn't Enough

Sometimes genuine conflict exists that can't be resolved through better communication:

**1. Actual Contract Violations**

- Recognition: The BA is correctly enforcing clear contract language that you're violating
- Response: Follow the contract or negotiate a change through Article XXVI (Local Option)
- Don't: Try to convince them the contract doesn't mean what it says

**2. Irreconcilable Operational Needs**

- Recognition: Customer requirements genuinely conflict with contract provisions
- Response: Escalate to Regional Field Manager and IUEC Regional Director
- Document: What customer requires, what contract says, what accommodation would work

**3. Political Constraints on BA**

- Recognition: BA personally agrees with you but can't politically deliver
- Response: Understand what would give them cover—involve International Rep, document process, create formal record
- Don't: Put them in impossible position with their members

**4. Bad Faith Actors**

- Recognition: Rare, but some individuals operate from manipulation
- Response: Document everything, use formal grievance process, escalate to higher levels on both sides
- Note: This is legitimately rare. Most "bad faith" is actually constraint-based behavior you haven't understood yet.

## Section 6: Building Capacity Over Time

**Monthly Check-ins**

- Purpose: Not problem-solving, just information sharing
- Format: "Here's what I'm seeing coming up. What are you hearing from members?"
- Benefit: Problems get addressed before they become grievances

**Joint Problem-Solving Sessions**

- When: After resolving a difficult issue
- Question: "What made that work? How do we do more of that?"
- Document: Informal notes about what approaches succeeded

**Safety Committee Participation**

- Per Article XXVIII: Joint Labor-Management Safety Committee
- Opportunity: Build trust around shared interest (nobody wants injuries)
- Approach: Actually implement safety recommendations jointly developed

**Transparency About Constraints**

- Share: "Here's why I can't do what you're asking" (operational, customer, safety, legal)
- Ask: "What constraint are you operating under?"
- Find: Solutions that respect both sets of limits

## Section 7: Recognition Infrastructure Failure Modes

**Mistake 1: Performing Agreement**

- Looks like: "Sure, whatever you want" (when you actually disagree)
- Why it fails: Creates false common ground, problems resurface later
- Correction: Name the actual disagreement, work with it

**Mistake 2: Softening Boundaries**

- Looks like: "Well, maybe we could..." (when you know you can't)
- Why it fails: Creates unrealistic expectations
- Correction: "This is my actual constraint. It's real."

**Mistake 3: Transactional Completion**

- Looks like: Solving immediate problem, never discussing pattern
- Why it fails: Same issue keeps recurring
- Correction: "This worked this time. Should we document the approach?"

**Mistake 4: Premature Resolution**

- Looks like: Jumping to solution before understanding constraint
- Why it fails: Your solution doesn't address their actual concern
- Correction: Stay in the question longer. "What specifically are you worried about?"

## Section 8: Practical Tools & Documentation

### Conversation Preparation Template

Before meeting with BA, answer:

1. What am I actually trying to accomplish? (not "win" but actual operational need)
2. What contract article applies?
3. What constraint is the BA likely operating under?
4. What would count as success for both of us?
5. What information do I need from them?

### Documentation Standard

For any significant interaction:

- Date, time, who was present
- Issue discussed
- Contract article referenced
- What each party said their constraint was
- Solution agreed to (or next step if not resolved)
- Any commitments made by either side

Note: This isn't "building a case"—it's making agreements visible

### Escalation Decision Tree

Before escalating to Regional/International level:

- Have I named my actual constraint clearly?
- Have I understood their actual constraint?
- Have I proposed a solution that addresses both?
- Is there a contract interpretation question that needs clarification?
- Is there a systemic issue beyond this individual case?

If yes to last two → escalate. If no → keep working at local level.

## Section 9: Integrating with Company Culture

**If Your Company Culture is Adversarial:**

You'll face pressure to: not "give away" anything, "win" interactions, be suspicious of BA motives.

Recognition Infrastructure doesn't mean being weak. It means being effective. Document what works. When collaborative approaches succeed, make results visible to leadership.

**If Your Company Culture is Accommodating:**

You'll face pressure to: avoid all conflict, give in to keep peace, not enforce contract provisions that favor company.

Recognition Infrastructure doesn't mean avoiding necessary tension. It means making real constraints visible. Your job is to represent actual operational needs, not perform harmony.

## Section 10: What Success Looks Like

**Six Months In:**

- BA calls you before filing grievances to discuss
- You call BA when considering policy changes that affect members
- Fewer surprises on both sides
- Informal problem-solving before formal processes

**One Year In:**

- Joint safety committee actually functioning
- Local option agreements getting updated collaboratively
- Other field managers asking how you maintain good relationship
- BA tells their members "talk to [your name], they're reasonable"

**Long Term:**

- Contract negotiations informed by what actually works on ground
- Fewer grievances, faster resolution when they occur
- Safety issues addressed before they become incidents
- Trust sufficient to handle novel situations outside contract specifics

## Conclusion: The Pattern in Practice

Recognition Infrastructure in union relations means:

- Contract conflicts are information about system constraints
- BA "resistance" often indicates constraint you haven't understood
- Making limits visible enables joint problem-solving
- Every interaction either builds or degrades future capacity
- Your job is coherent operation within the structure, not defeating the other side

This approach doesn't guarantee you get everything you want. It guarantees you understand what's actually possible, and build capacity for future cooperation.

The goal isn't to eliminate conflict—some is structural and legitimate. The goal is to ensure conflict happens over real differences, not misunderstanding and defensive positioning.

## Appendix: Quick Reference Cards

### When BA Says This → What They Might Actually Mean

**"This violates the contract"** → Could mean:

- It actually does (check article they cite)
- It sets precedent they're worried about
- They need formal process to show members they fought
- They don't understand your operational constraint

**"Members won't accept this"** → Could mean:

- True political constraint they're operating under
- They personally disagree but need cover
- They haven't explained it clearly to members yet
- They're using members as reason to avoid uncomfortable decision

**"We'll grieve this"** → Could mean:

- Actual contract violation they must address
- They need to show members they're taking action
- They want formal record of company position
- They're hoping you'll settle before formal process

**"That's not past practice"** → Could mean:

- There's precedent you're breaking
- Change creates uncertainty they're managing
- New approach advantages company unfairly
- They need transition time to prepare members

**Final Notes:**

This guide assumes good faith on both sides. If you encounter actual bad faith actors (rare), standard labor relations procedures apply. But check first: what looks like bad faith is usually someone operating under constraints you don't understand yet.

The Recognition Infrastructure approach doesn't replace labor relations expertise—it enhances it by adding a layer of systems thinking to traditional adversarial models.

Questions or case studies to discuss: This framework is meant to be applied, not just read. Bring specific situations to your labor relations team, work through them using this lens.
